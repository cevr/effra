# Managed Go runtime

The native prototype uses `runtime/effra`, a Go-standard-library runtime with typed lazy closures and managed goroutines. The JS target emits pinned Effect with a small ownership policy over Effect fibers, scopes and causes. Shared conformance tests establish child-before-parent cleanup, unobserved failure propagation, and timeout cleanup-defect preservation. Go imports, Files and Runtime remain Go-only (EF110); Http/LiveHttp runs on both targets. This is a tested common lifecycle subset, not complete provider parity.

## Lifecycle contract

`scope { ... }` executes a block inside a new owning scope and preserves its result after cleanup. A scope goes Open → Closing → Closed: stop admissions, request child cancellation, complete adapter abort hooks and admitted acquisitions, wait for owned child cleanup, then release resources in reverse acquisition order. A successful acquisition that finishes during closure is released once before closure completes.

`scope { body }` executes its body under a fresh owning scope. Its non-recipe result becomes visible to the caller only after owned child shutdown and resource cleanup complete. Failures, defects, interruption and accompanying cleanup causes retain the ordinary scope contract.

A final expression whose value is a recipe is not implicitly executed and is not admitted as a top-level scope result. It reports EF105 with a scope-specific explanation and a suggested `run` edit. Binding an unused recipe does not execute it.

`run expression` executes exactly one recipe layer under the current scope, contributes that execution’s failure and service rows, and yields its success value. If that success value is another recipe, it remains a recipe; executing it requires another explicit `run`. The scope-tail rule still applies.

No returned value or transitive capture may retain a managed resource owned by the closing scope. Proven outer-owner borrows remain valid; unresolved managed ownership is handled conservatively. Safe inert recipes carried in checked data are not implicitly executed or blanket-rejected. Recipe-result function slots and branch value selection retain their existing rules.

`fork recipe()` admits a child before starting its goroutine. Its service context is inherited lexically. `run child.join()` awaits its result, `run child.cancel()` requests cancellation, and `run child.interrupt()` requests cancellation and awaits completed cleanup. Normal interruption is acknowledged by interrupt; real child failures/cleanup defects are preserved. Unobserved child failures propagate at owner closure. The checker does not charge a child's failure row at the `fork`: the failure is pending from the fork until a completed `join` or `interrupt` of that child observes it, on every path to an exit, and `recover` and `catch` do not observe it. A pending failure is raised as an ordinary failure at the owner edge that closes over the child (a `scope`, a fork child, a `timeout`, a provision or the root), so the function containing that edge must declare it (EF107 otherwise); returning, branching on or running a fiber keeps its evidence, while carrying it through a parameter, payload, joined result or callable contract is refused. The runtime raises an ordinary failure only when exactly one failure reaches the edge. On a close that is not cancellation-aborted, the body's failure together with an unobserved child's and the failures of two children reach the edge as one composite cause, which no handler matches and no row can declare; the checker refuses the function whose owner would raise it (EF107, `composite cause`). Join or interrupt the child first. The root is the exception: it permits a composite whose typed components `main` declares, and the process exits 1 like any declared failure.

At the transition from Open to Closing, an owner is cancellation-aborted if its body exit contains interruption or cancellation has already been requested on its executing fiber. This classification is fixed before close requests child cancellation; later requests do not change it. A cancellation-aborted owner waits for all owned shutdown and cleanup, but discards the ordinary typed failures of children it has not observed. Defects and cleanup failures are preserved. The owner's own body exit is preserved. This rule applies at every nested owner. On a non-aborted close, unobserved child failures continue to propagate. Abandoned typed failures are not delivered to a handler, caller or diagnostic channel, so the interruption of a running fiber, a scope closed over a cancelled fiber and a timeout which fires over unobserved children are the cancellation alone, never a composite with a child failure. Effect 4.0.1 drops unobserved child failures on every exit; Effra drops them only here and keeps them on a normal close. Eio preserves a real failure even while its switch is cancelling and drops only `Cancelled`; Effra's fork is latent, so a child which has not been observed has no observer to report to once its owner is abandoned.

`.timeout(ms)` is lazy, adds `Timeout` and `Scheduler`, and runs inside an owned deadline scope. It waits for the operation's shutdown and cleanup, so elapsed time can exceed the deadline. When the deadline wins, the timed computation is abandoned: its ordinary typed failures are discarded, while defects and cleanup failures are preserved. A successful deadline produces `Timeout`; a failed deadline preserves its selected cause. Both outcomes await the timed computation's shutdown and cleanup. Interruption of an already-completed child still re-raises that child's retained non-interruption failure. A child which fails at the instant of the deadline is a race between two single declared causes (the failure if the work wins, `Timeout` if the deadline does); the outcome is bounded by the declared row and is not a composite. The targets may pick different winners of that race (Go takes either branch when both are ready; the JS deadline has been observed to win every time), and a program must not depend on which. In live Go execution it uses the process timer driver; in tests, the explicit scheduler driver controls both `Clock.sleep` and timeout deadlines. A provider that implements only `Clock.sleep` cannot control a timeout. Durations must be 0–2,147,483,647 milliseconds; invalid runtime durations are defects.

`Sync.latch()` creates an opaque, one-shot `Latch`. `Sync.signal(latch)` is idempotent, and `Sync.await(latch)` scopes cancellation to the waiting fiber. A nil handle is an invalid-latch defect.

Latch handoff has one meaning wherever execution is serialized into turns: every JavaScript execution, and Go under the managed test scheduler (`ef test`, the Go `TestScheduler`). `Sync.signal` completes the latch, makes every parked waiter runnable and returns. It never runs a waiter. The signaller keeps its turn until it next suspends or exits, and each waiter resumes later, on a turn of its own. On JavaScript the operation budget can also end a turn by preempting the fiber. Four rules follow:

1. A waiter never runs inside its signaller's turn. The order among several woken waiters, and between them and other runnable fibers, is unspecified.
2. The signal is complete when `signal` returns. Interrupting the signaller afterwards, directly or by closing its scope, cannot undo the signal or reach a waiter it does not own. A waiter the signaller or the closing scope owns is cancelled by ownership, like any owned fiber, and rule 4 then applies to it. Otherwise the interrupt reaches only the signaller's own later work.
3. A signalled waiter is runnable until it runs, so it holds off [quiescence](../GLOSSARY.md#tooling-and-testing).
4. A waiter interrupted after it is signalled but before its turn ends interrupted, whether the interrupt is direct or comes from closing its owner. It performs no further effect, and a join reports the interruption. The latch stays signalled for every other waiter and for later awaits. Awaiting a signalled latch returns at once without giving up the turn.

Live Go, with no test scheduler, has no turns: a managed latch wait is a plain select on the latch and the fiber's context, and fibers run on their own goroutines in parallel. There, rule 2 holds, a waiter never runs inside its signaller's call, and a waiter whose context is cancelled before its next checkpoint ends interrupted there. But the signaller does not keep a turn, so a waiter can run alongside the signaller, and an interrupt sent after `signal` races with the waiter. The waiter may already have acted. A program that needs rule 4 in live Go must order the interrupt before the signal. Rule 3 does not apply: quiescence exists only under the test scheduler.

Under the test scheduler, the Go managed signal reserves one wake per waiter and closes its handoff channel. The waiter's goroutine takes the turn only after the signaller releases it. A direct interrupt cancels the target before the interrupter hands off its turn. A scope close moves the scope to Closing, fixes its abort classification and cancels the scope and its children while the closer still holds the turn, and it hands the turn off only for the shutdown waits and cleanup. This covers a `scope` block, a fiber's exit and layer shutdown. A woken waiter therefore finds its context cancelled when it takes the turn, and ends interrupted at its next checkpoint, at the latest when the fiber exits (`Invoke`). On JavaScript, Effra's `Latch` is Effect's `Latch`. Its effectful `open` moves the parked waiters onto one dispatcher task and returns, the same path a `yieldNow` takes. Interrupting a waiter before that task runs removes it from the task, and Effect interrupts a suspended fiber at once. Effect's `openUnsafe` and `Deferred` completion resume their waiters inline, inside the completer's turn, so Effra uses neither for a latch. Go channels and `sync.Cond`, Trio's `Event.set`, Kotlin's `CompletableDeferred` under a dispatcher, and BEAM message send make the same choice: waking makes the waiter runnable, and it runs on its own schedule. [latch-handoff.ef](../examples/latch-handoff.ef) pins the four rules on both targets under `ef test`.

The Go `TestScheduler` exposes `AwaitNextRegistration` and strong `Adjust` operations: each deadline selection and target commit observes activity, wake reservations and runnable cleanup/publication continuations atomically. A runnable managed continuation blocks advancement; a continuation parked on a registered virtual timer or managed signal may remain pending, so a partial adjustment can return at its target before cleanup completes. A later adjustment or signal resumes that cleanup, while one adjustment drains every reachable intermediate deadline. Only managed waits participate in this observation; unmanaged goroutines and foreign blocking calls remain outside the virtual scheduler and can delay completion. `Advance` remains a compatibility alias for `Adjust`.

`Scheduler.awaitRegistration()` returns at [quiescence](../GLOSSARY.md#tooling-and-testing), with one meaning on both targets: no managed fiber is running or runnable, and a wake in flight is not idle. Each live managed fiber is then parked on a registered virtual timer, a latch or a join, or has exited, so every timer that managed work can register without outside input is registered. The barrier is level-triggered on that state, not on a new registration. Forked work that already completed passes it, as does work whose timeout timer was registered and then cancelled, or never started because Effect runs no body of a fiber interrupted before its first turn. `Scheduler.advance` selects each deadline at the same quiescence. When several fibers wait at the barrier or in `advance` at once, the order in which they are released is unspecified, and the targets differ: JavaScript resumes one waiter at a time, while Go releases every waiter on the same state change. Go `synctest` forbids concurrent waits, and Trio wakes waiters by cushion, not arrival. A test must not depend on the order. Interrupting a fiber parked at the barrier is an ordinary interruption on both targets, never a defect.

On Go, `Adjust` and the barrier share one predicate. No fiber holds the turn or is admitted and waiting for it. No timer, signal or join wake is reserved, and no cleanup continuation is runnable. No parked wait has a cancelled context. Every managed wait that also selects on its context goes through one helper, `FiberContext.parkCancellable`, which holds both cancellation rules. A cancellation from any source (an interrupt, a scope close, a timeout or an ancestor's cancellation) reaches every descendant context at once, without a wake reservation. The parked wait therefore counts as runnable from the moment its context is done until the fiber has its turn back. A wait that ignores its context, such as an interrupter waiting for its target's completion, does not count, so cleanup parked on a future timer cannot hold a partial adjustment. A wait woken by cancellation ends in an interrupt cause carrying the context's error, so interrupting a sleeper, a latch waiter, a joiner or a barrier waiter is never a defect. Only a nil, closed or inactive scheduler is a defect at the barrier. The barrier's caller parks its own turn and continuation first.

On JavaScript, every managed fiber runs on the harness dispatcher: forks, yields, preempted continuations and latch handoffs are queued tasks. A callback resumption (a fired timer, an interruption, a fiber's completion) runs its fiber inline, on top of the resumer's still-running turn, so an empty queue proves nothing while a turn is on the stack. Quiescence is decided only by the dispatcher's top-level drain, which runs from a microtask with no fiber turn beneath it. The barrier and each deadline selection of `advance` suspend their fiber as a quiescence waiter. The drain resumes one waiter only when the queue is empty, and drains whatever that resumption made runnable before the next waiter. Effect's own synchronous `flush` runs queued tasks but never decides quiescence.

As for adjustment, unmanaged goroutines, host promises and foreign blocking calls are not observed. The host Go method `TestScheduler.AwaitNextRegistration` is a different, edge-triggered handshake. A test goroutine that starts managed roots cannot see them admitted, so the method waits for a timer registration since the last adjustment or host handshake instead.

Cancellation is cooperative. Managed waits and generated effect boundaries observe Go context. Pure CPU work and blocking foreign APIs may delay shutdown. Go acquisition/registration and both targets’ scope finalizers mask logical cancellation; foreign acquisition must eventually return. An adapter can register an idempotent `Scope.OnCancel` hook before blocking work to unblock an API that needs an explicit close/abort. Hooks must return and must not close or wait on their own owning scope.

Exits preserve named failures, defects and interruption separately, including additional cleanup/child causes. `.catch<Tag>(value)` handles a lone matching failure; it does not erase accompanying cleanup defects. Managed panics become defects while cleanup runs. Fatal runtime/process termination and unmanaged goroutine panics are outside this protocol.

## Recursion depth

A pure function whose self call is in tail position runs in constant stack on both targets: Go emits a `for` loop and JavaScript a `while (true)` loop, so a recursion that is a million calls deep behaves the same on Go, Bun and Node. Non-tail recursion, mutual recursion and recursion through `run` are ordinary calls and are bounded by the host. Go grows a goroutine stack to 1 GB and then aborts the process; V8 raises `RangeError` after roughly ten thousand frames. A recursion through `run self()` that is a million calls deep completes on JavaScript (Effect.gen keeps its frames on the heap) but exceeds the Go stack limit, so portable programs should not rely on deep effect recursion.

## Small standard library

| Service / provider | Operations | Contract |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Console / Stdout | `log(string) -> void` | Explicit console capability |
| Clock / LiveClock | `sleep(i64) -> void` | Cancellation-aware millisecond wait; JS uses bigint input |
| Scheduler / LiveScheduler | `sleep(i64) -> void`, `advance(i64) -> void`, `awaitRegistration() -> void` | Explicit deadline authority; `TestScheduler` controls virtual sleeps/adjustment and `LiveScheduler` is only valid for live execution |
| Sync / TestSync | `latch() -> Latch`, `await(Latch) -> void`, `signal(Latch) -> void` | Shared one-shot synchronization; waiter interruption does not consume the handle |
| Env / LiveEnv | `get(string) -> string` | Empty string for absent values; this is not a presence test |
| Files / LiveFiles | `openRead(string) -> File`, `readText(File) -> string`, `readFile(string) -> string` | IoError; openRead attaches release to the current scope; readFile opens a narrower scope |
| Http / LiveHttp | `listen(string, HttpLimits, HttpHandler) -> void`, `text(string) -> bytes` | IoError; owns listener, bounds requests, and waits for request cleanup on shutdown. Go and JS |
| Runtime / RuntimeLive | `inspect() -> string` | JSON metadata for the current owning scope |

Files use synchronized managed handles; using a handle after its owner closes produces IoError. This is a runtime guard, not region typing or proof against every mutable alias. Native reads are ordinary blocking Go file reads and may delay cancellation. File reading currently buffers the entire content; streaming/bounded I/O remains future work.

Runtime snapshots report scope ID/state, acquisition count, cancellation request, resource count/labels, and child states Running/Cancelling/Done. Child completion means its cleanup completed. Lists are limited to 100 entries, labels to 256 bytes, and truncation is reported. Closed scopes clear owned references. Snapshots are observational, not an atomic freeze of all tasks; labels may identify application resources. No payload dump, process-wide task traversal, wait-reason tracing, or MCP runtime control is exposed.

Try `./bin/ef run examples/lifecycle.ef`. Each provider remains explicit and replaceable through the same nominal service interface used by user implementations.

## Go interop seam

At the Go runtime interface, `FromGo(func(context.Context) (A,error))` constructs a deferred call. It passes the managed context and preserves both returned values in `GoResult[A]`; a returned error is initially native data. The adapter must honestly state whether its API observes context. No goroutine is spawned just to hide a blocking call, and no signature is treated as proof of cancellation or purity.

`OrFail(recipe)` explicitly adapts the returned error to GoError. Its payload contains both the native error and partial value. A panic remains a defect; observed context cancellation remains interruption. Resource-owning APIs use `AcquireRelease`, whose acquisition error is currently classified as IoError in this prototype; more general domain acquisition policies remain future work. APIs needing an abort hook register `OnCancel` before admission to blocking work.

The runnable [Go example](../examples/go-interop/main.go) calls real `io.ReadFull`, retaining its partial count and `unexpected EOF`, then demonstrates explicit error adaptation and a context-aware cancellation boundary:

```sh
go run ./examples/go-interop
# raw: count=3 data="abc" error=unexpected EOF
# adapted: GoError partial=3 error=unexpected EOF
# cancelled SDK: Timeout
```

Automatic source imports now consume Go export data for primitive package functions. Foreign/Host is the explicit capability for these deferred calls. See [interop](interop.md) and [imports example](../examples/imports.ef). Imported methods follow Go's method sets (see [interop](interop.md) and [host types example](../examples/host-types.ef)); native fields, method values and persistent signature caches remain unsupported.

## HTTP server

`Http.listen(address, limits, handler)` is the only builtin HTTP server operation on Go and server JavaScript; every header-read, body-read, idle, body-size and active-exchange limit is explicit and no raw operation bypasses them. Handler service requirements flow into the server recipe, the transport records its absorbed typed failures, and listener/startup failures remain `IoError`.

Listen addresses and listener failures are Effra-owned text, identical on Go and JavaScript (`parseHTTPAddress` and `httpListenError` in `runtime/effra/http.go`, `__ef_http_address` and `__ef_http_listen_error` in `internal/compiler/prelude/http.mjs`). An address is `host:port`:

- The port is 0 to 65535 in decimal. Port 0 lets the OS choose. A service name such as `http` or an empty port is refused.
- The host is empty (every interface), a name or IPv4 address without a colon, or an IPv6 address in brackets, such as `[::1]:8080`.

Any other address fails with `IoError` before the host sees it, such as `invalid HTTP address "bad": missing port`. Other reasons are `unbalanced brackets`, `an IPv6 host must be in brackets`, `brackets must enclose an IPv6 address` and `port must be a decimal number from 0 to 65535`. A host listener failure is reported as `HTTP listen on "ADDRESS": CLASS`. The classes are:

- `address in use` (EADDRINUSE)
- `address not available` (EADDRNOTAVAIL)
- `permission denied` (EACCES or EPERM)
- `host lookup failed`: the host name does not resolve. Both targets look a name up exactly once and bind the first IPv4 answer, or the first answer when there is no IPv4 one. Go's `net.Listen` does this itself (Go 1.27 `addrs.first(isIPv4)`, net/ipsock.go). IPv4 means what Go's `IP.To4` accepts, so an IPv4-mapped IPv6 answer such as `::ffff:127.0.0.2` counts as IPv4 and binds `127.0.0.2`, and so does a mapped literal such as `[::ffff:127.0.0.2]:0`. A host or a lookup answer is read as a numeric address the way Go's `netip.ParseAddr` reads it (`__ef_http_parse_addr` on JavaScript). The zone is split off first, the address part is classified, and a zone must be non-empty and on an IPv6 address. Anything else is a name. A zoned mapped address such as `[::ffff:7f00:2%lo_0]:0` therefore binds `127.0.0.2` and drops the zone, as Go ignores it for an IPv4 socket. A genuine IPv6 address keeps its zone. `[::1%]:0` and `127.0.0.1%0:0` are looked up as names and fail with `host lookup failed` on both targets.

Known divergence: binding a genuine IPv6 address with a zone, such as a link-local `fe80::1%eth0`, depends on the host. Go maps a zone name to its interface index and falls back to reading the zone as a decimal index (net/interface.go). Node on Unix accepts interface names, and `listen` treats a zone it cannot parse as a host name. Both targets select the same address and zone, but whether it binds can differ. JavaScript resolves in the adapter and passes the numeric answer to `listen`, so the host never resolves again. That matters because Node reports a failed lookup as `getaddrinfo ENOTFOUND` while Bun reports `listen ENOENT` or `EAGAIN`. The order of the answers is the resolver's own (Go's resolver or the system's `getaddrinfo`), so a name with several IPv4 answers may bind a different one on each target.

Any other listener failure keeps the host's text after the prefix, and is the one host-specific entry failure report. Go wraps errors in `net.OpError` with its own text (`listen tcp ...: bind: address already in use`), and Node exposes a portable `code` beside host text, so the classes key on errno and lookup errors rather than on text. Erlang's `:gen_tcp.listen` returns POSIX atoms such as `{:error, :eaddrinuse}` and formats them separately (`:inet.format_error/1`), which is the model followed here. The WASI sockets interface used by MoonBit's WASI examples has the same small set (`address-in-use`, `address-not-bindable`, `access-denied`, `name-unresolvable`, `unknown`). Before 2026-10-09, Go passed `net.Listen` text through and JavaScript raised a defect for an address without a colon.

Run `./bin/ef run examples/http.ef` from the repository. It prints `listening http://127.0.0.1:PORT`; port zero lets the OS select an available port. Use that URL with `/health`, `/users/42`, `/users/slow`, `/users/missing`, or `/file`. Its handler answers each route with a `text/plain; charset=utf-8` `HttpReply.Respond` under literal limits for its workload (no request bodies, five-second reads); a declared `GoError` becomes an empty 500.

Each request executes inside a fresh managed scope, with cancellation linked to its connection and server lifetime. SIGTERM stops admission, cancels requests, and waits for handlers and their cleanup before returning. A foreign call ignoring cancellation can delay shutdown; there is no detached timeout escape. An interrupted program writes the entry failure report `interrupt` and exits with status 130 on Go and JavaScript (see [the design](design.md#entry-failure-report)).

Breaking change (2026-10-07): the raw path-to-text `Http.serve(address, handler)`, its host adapters and the `Handler` shorthand were removed without a compatibility facade; a call to `Http.serve` is EF102 and `Handler` is an ordinary name. Migrating to `Http.listen` intentionally changes behaviour: the handler receives the request path with its percent-encoding kept; oversized bodies and exhausted capacity are answered 413 and 503 without running the handler; failure responses (500) have an empty body and no Content-Type; a request cancelled by shutdown receives 503 after its scope closed.

### Managed transport

`Http.listen(address, limits, handler)` is the bounded buffered HTTP/1.1 transport; [examples/http-transport.ef](../examples/http-transport.ef) is the public program. A reference to the global `Http` or `LiveHttp` (a service row, `provide<Http>`, an operation call or the provider value), as resolved by the checker's single binding phase before builtin data is registered, admits these builtin records. A local value or row parameter of either name is not a reference anywhere in its scope, including explicit constructor types. Only checked uses of `LiveHttp` emit the transport implementation:

```effra
record HttpRequest { method: string, path: string, contentType: string, body: bytes }
record HttpResponse { status: i64, contentType: string, body: bytes }
enum HttpReply { Respond { response: HttpResponse }, BadRequest, NotFound, UnsupportedMediaType }
record HttpLimits { maxBodyBytes: i64, readHeaderMillis: i64, readBodyMillis: i64, idleMillis: i64, maxActive: i64 }
```

The handler is an effect function `HttpRequest -> HttpReply`. Its service requirements flow into the server recipe and its typed failures are absorbed by the transport (inspection reports them as `AbsorbedFailures`). `path` is the request-target path exactly as received, without query or percent-decoding; for an absolute-form target it is the path after the authority (`/` when empty), and the query is removed first. `contentType` is empty when absent. Every limit is required; timeouts must lie in 1..2147483647 ms, `maxActive` in 1..2147483647 and `maxBodyBytes` in 0..2^53-1, otherwise the recipe dies before binding.

| Condition | Response |
| --- | --- |
| `Respond` | its status (200..599), body and Content-Type (omitted when empty; never sniffed) |
| `BadRequest` / `NotFound` / `UnsupportedMediaType` | 400 / 404 / 415, empty body, no Content-Type |
| admitted requests at `maxActive`, or shutdown begun | 503, `Connection: close`, handler not run |
| declared or chunked body over `maxBodyBytes` | 413, `Connection: close`, handler not run |
| malformed body framing | 400, `Connection: close`, handler not run |
| headers or body not received in time | connection closed without a response |
| handler failure, defect, cleanup failure, or invalid response | 500, empty body |
| `Respond` Content-Type outside visible ASCII, space and tab | 500, empty body (invalid response) |
| server shutdown cancels the handler | 503, `Connection: close`, after the request scope closed |
| client disconnect | handler cancelled; no further bytes |

A request holds its admission from body read until its response has been handed to the operating system or its connection closed, so `maxActive` also bounds buffered responses a slow client has not received. Admission rejects at the cap and never waits, so a sequential client may observe 503 while the previous response is still being handed off. This includes a request with malformed body framing: its body timer stops, its admission lasts until its 400 has drained or the connection closed, and the transport then closes the connection itself, so a client that keeps its side open cannot hold shutdown open. Go serves a connection's requests in order, so that 400 follows every earlier pipelined response. On JS a parser error (malformed headers or body framing) is answered directly on the socket: the 400 follows only response bytes already written, and an earlier pipelined response not yet written is never sent, whether its request arrived in the same packet or an earlier one; that request's handler is cancelled as on a client disconnect. This is a known JS host limit. Shutdown stops admission, cancels and joins every request scope, then gives responses still being written `idleMillis` to complete before aborting their connections, and finally closes the listener. The grace is a separate transport-drain phase: it starts once the last request scope has closed, never when only the writer's own scope has, so no abort overlaps another request's cleanup. A client that stops reading therefore never holds shutdown open, and owned cleanup always completes before any transport abort. Go closes the listener at cancellation; JS keeps it open, answering 503, until the drain ends, because `node:http`'s `close()` would also destroy responses still being written. Bun's `node:http` accepts a whole response at once, so on Bun these response bounds are Bun's own buffering.

The Content-Type policy (visible ASCII, space and horizontal tab) is the intersection of what Go and Node publish unchanged; validation precedes any byte of the response, and a host rejection during publication becomes a 500 (or aborts a response already started) instead of escaping the transport.

Routing, method selection and media-type policy belong to the handler; the example answers a wrong method on a known path with 404, matching the selected profile. Every response is written only after the request scope, including handler resources and children, has closed, so a cleanup failure is never published as success. On JS the transport uses `node:http` (Node or Bun) with Effect fibers; the generated entry interrupts `main` on SIGINT/SIGTERM. General headers, query parameters and streaming bodies remain future work.

### Typed endpoints

An endpoint derived with `Http.endpoint` ([declaration](bundled-modules.md#failure-witnesses-and-typed-endpoints)) applies one shared buffered JSON profile, implemented once per target (`goEndpointHelpers`, `prelude/endpoint.mjs`) over the transport above:

- another method or path answers 404; a route table (`Http.routes`) dispatches on exact method and path, so a wrong method also answers 404, the pinned Effect profile;
- with input, a Content-Type whose media type is not `application/json` (case-insensitive, parameters admitted) answers 415; an absent Content-Type reads as JSON, as pinned Effect's `HttpApiBuilder` does. The body is decoded once by the input witness, structure first; a structural failure, including a body that is not UTF-8, answers an empty 400 before any transformation or handler runs;
- success encodes the handler's result with the output witness, `Content-Type: application/json`, at the declared status; an endpoint without output answers its status with no body;
- a mapped failure answers its status, with a body from its failure witness if one is named. It is mapped only when it is the whole cause: a compound cause, such as another mapped failure from an unobserved child, a defect, an interruption or a cleanup cause beside it, propagates complete, so the endpoint's row keeps every mapped failure;
- a failed success encoding propagates `JsonEncodeFailure` and a failed failure-body encoding is terminal: it is never mapped again and its cause keeps the mapped failure after the encoding failure (`JsonEncodeFailure; Unpublishable`). The transport answers either with its generic 500, after request-scope cleanup like any response.

The shared wire corpus `conformance/http/endpoint-vectors.json` records these exchanges and runs on Go, Bun and Node.
